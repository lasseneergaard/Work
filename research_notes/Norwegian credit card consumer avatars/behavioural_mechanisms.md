## 1. Present bias / hyperbolic discounting and credit card borrowing

### Takeaway
Strong international evidence links present bias to holding and failing to pay down card debt. Danish transaction data link it to high-cost borrowing, and one Norwegian bank-data PhD links "low concern for future consequences" to card delinquency. Applicability to Norway is **INFERRED, close to VERIFIED for a minority of revolvers and high-cost borrowers (medium-high confidence)**. It is not a description of the typical Norwegian cardholder, most of whom pay before interest accrues (see §12).

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** Present bias (quasi-hyperbolic, β-δ discounting) means putting disproportionate weight on immediate costs and benefits compared with all future ones. It produces plans the person later fails to keep. "Naive" agents do not foresee their own future impatience; "sophisticated" agents do. Kuchler & Pagel model the difference: only sophisticates' spending responds to available resources, and sophisticates' planned paydown predicts actual paydown better than naives'. — [Kuchler & Pagel 2021, JFE 139(2):359–388, RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:139:y:2021:i:2:p:359-388); [NBER w24881](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24881) [OTHER][S]
- **Meier & Sprenger (2010), "Present-Biased Preferences and Credit Card Borrowing", *AEJ: Applied Economics* 2(1):193–210, DOI [10.1257/app.2.1.193](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.2.1.193).** US low-to-moderate-income individuals. Incentivised time-preference choice experiments were matched to credit reports and tax returns. Present-biased individuals were about **15 percentage points more likely to hold any credit card debt**, and conditional on borrowing held about **25% larger balances**. Results held after controls for disposable income, socio-demographics and credit constraints. Limitations: one low-to-moderate-income US sample recruited at tax-preparation sites (exact N not verified in-session), cross-sectional. — [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:2:y:2010:i:1:p:193-210); [typeset full-text copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/present-biased-preferences-and-credit-card-borrowing-3r0hpurpex.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Kuchler & Pagel (2021), "Sticking to Your Plan: The Role of Present Bias for Credit Card Paydown", *Journal of Financial Economics* 139(2):359–388.** US users of an online debt-paydown service, with high-frequency data on income, spending, balances and limits. Design: observational. Paycheck sensitivity of spending is used to identify short-run impatience. Many consumers **do not stick to the paydown plans they set themselves**, and present bias explains this best. Sophisticates' average paydown falls as measured impatience rises. Limitation: a self-selected sample of people who signed up to a debt-paydown tool. — [Columbia PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25556/KuchlerPagel_StickingToYourPlan.pdf); [NBER w24881](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24881) [OTHER][S]
- **Shui & Ausubel (2004/2005), "Time Inconsistency in the Credit Card Market"** (University of Maryland working paper/dissertation). US randomised mail-offer experiment by an issuer. More consumers accepted a **lower teaser rate with a shorter duration** than a higher teaser with a longer duration, although their later borrowing showed the longer offer would have been cheaper. The structural estimate of the present-bias factor is **β ≈ 0.8**, with average switching cost ≈ **$150**. Limitations: unpublished working paper, and the estimates depend on the structural model's assumptions. — [UMD DRUM record](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/b3bc4c71-9c90-4dfe-9ddb-dbfd108a0296); [Berkeley course copy](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/dellavigna/e218_f05/ausubel.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Hansen (2022), "What drives the demand for high-cost consumption loans?", CEBI Working Paper 07/22, University of Copenhagen.** Danske Bank transaction data (Denmark's largest bank); observational. Uses the Kuchler-Pagel paycheck-sensitivity proxy. **High-cost borrowers appear significantly more present-biased than other consumers** after controls for age, gender, income and liquidity. They spend heavily on **gambling** before and at first borrowing, persistently spend more than they earn (the gap widens before the first loan), and show **no evidence of expenditure, health or social shocks** at borrowing. The author reads this as self-control problems. Limitations: paycheck sensitivity is also consistent with liquidity constraints; the paper covers high-cost "quick loans", not credit cards; it is a working paper. — [CEBI WP 07/22 PDF](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/publikationer/working-papers/CEBI_WP_07-22.rev.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kud:kucebi:2207); [CEBI news, July 2022](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/news/users-of-high-cost-loans-have-personality-traits-in-common) [NORDIC][S]
- **Epper, Fehr, Fehr-Duda, Kreiner, Lassen, Leth-Petersen & Rasmussen (2020), "Time Discounting and Wealth Inequality", *American Economic Review* 110(4):1177–1205** (DOI [10.1257/aer.20181096](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20181096), [BK]). Incentivised patience measures for people born 1973–83 who lived in Copenhagen at age 7, matched to Danish administrative registers. There is a **strong positive correlation between patience and position in the wealth distribution**, robust to education, income profile, school grades, initial and parental wealth, credit constraints and risk preferences. Savings behaviour is the proposed channel. Limitation: the outcome is wealth, not card debt. — [CEBI WP 08/19](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/publikationer/working-papers/CEBI_WP_08-19.pdf); [ZORA record](https://www.zora.uzh.ch/entities/publication/3cef65ba-656e-4491-b603-91d6f0209b7a) [NORDIC][S]
- **Huse (2019), "Predicting Credit Card Delinquency: A Fundamental Model of Cardholder Financial Behavior", PhD thesis, NHH.** Combined credit- and debit-card transactions from **a Norwegian bank, June 2008–June 2011**; predictive modelling. The thesis tests mental-accounting and present-bias hypotheses. **Lower concern for future consequences raises delinquency risk**, and present-biased cardholders discount future repayments and keep spending at expensive rates. The model out-predicted common machine-learning credit-scoring algorithms. Related outputs: an EMAC 2020 paper (Huse, Haugland, Hunneman) and a 2025 *Journal of Finance and Data Science* follow-up. Limitations: one bank; pre-dates the 2019 debt register and lending regulation; predictive rather than causal; present bias is inferred from behaviour. — [NHH Open Access](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2640771); [NHH Bulletin, Nov 2019](https://nhh.no/en/nhh-bulletin/article-archive/2019/november/predicting-credit-card-delinquency/); [EMAC 2020 paper](https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/R2020-84962.pdf) [NO][S]
- **Uncertainty from liquidity.** In the UK field experiment, nudged cardholders chose autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum, and the authors attribute the absence of any debt reduction to **limited liquidity**. Some apparent "impatience" is therefore binding constraints. — [Guttman-Kenney et al. 2025, LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/130080) [OTHER][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: INFERRED with partial Nordic and Norwegian support, medium-high confidence.** Why: (a) the mechanism is a general property of human time preference, documented with US field data and Danish transaction and register data; (b) a Norwegian bank-data thesis finds a consistent pattern for card delinquency; (c) Danish banking and welfare institutions resemble Norway's. Caveat: no Norwegian study measures present bias directly (incentivised elicitation or paycheck sensitivity) and links it to card revolving.
- Who it applies to: a minority. Norwegian data show that non-interest-bearing card balances (paid before the due date) are growing while interest-bearing revolving balances trend down (§12). Present bias is therefore most relevant to the revolver and high-cost-borrower segment, not to "transactors".
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Ask a realistic self-assessment question before ranking: "In the last 12 months, how many times did you *not* pay the full bill?" Naive present-biased users underestimate future borrowing, so base the ranking on past behaviour rather than intentions.
  - Show the cost of carrying a balance (e.g., NOK 10,000 for 3, 6 and 12 months) next to rewards and benefits.
  - Promote commitment devices: autopay of the full balance, a lower credit limit, spending alerts.
  - Do not use urgency or immediate-gratification copy ("money in your account today"). That exploits the bias and may conflict with Norwegian credit-marketing rules (see §4, HYPOTHESIS on legal scope).

### Gaps
- No Norwegian study was found that elicits time preferences and links them to registry card debt. Norwegian register-plus-experiment work similar to Epper et al. would be needed.
- Laibson, Repetto & Tobacman ("A Debt Puzzle") and Laibson et al. ("Estimating discount functions with consumption choices over the lifecycle", NBER WP 13314) are canonical evidence. Their parameter estimates were not verified this session and are deliberately not quoted.
- Hansen (2022) was only read as an abstract. Effect sizes for the present-bias difference were not obtained.

## 2. Mental accounting, payment decoupling and the "pain of paying"

### Takeaway
The classic finding that credit cards raise willingness to pay (WTP) and impulse buying is **real but small and weakening**. The 2024 meta-analysis finds a small, declining "cashless effect" that depends on location, and at least one replication failed. A 32,000-person mega-replication supports a pain-of-paying effect mainly on amount spent, not WTP. In Norway the more relevant forms of mental accounting are **how people classify types of debt** and **decoupling of spending from repayment**. These have Norwegian support (SIFO survey; Huse thesis). Applicability: **VERIFIED (limited) for debt-type mental accounting and decoupling, medium confidence**. A credit-versus-debit spending premium in Norway is a **HYPOTHESIS (low confidence)**.

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions.** *Mental accounting*: people track money in separate, non-fungible mental "accounts" and judge costs and benefits within them. *Pain of paying*: the immediate hedonic cost of handing over payment. *Coupling/decoupling*: how closely in time and salience a payment is linked to the consumption it buys. Prelec & Loewenstein's "prospective accounting" model holds that paying in advance or at the same time as consumption ("coupling") raises the pain of paying, while deferred or aggregated payment (credit cards) decouples it. — [Prelec & Loewenstein 1998, "The Red and the Black: Mental Accounting of Savings and Debt", *Marketing Science* 17(1):4–28, DOI 10.1287/mksc.17.1.4](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.17.1.4) [OTHER][BK]
- **Prelec & Simester (2001), "Always Leave Home Without It: A Further Investigation of the Credit-Card Effect on Willingness to Pay", *Marketing Letters* 12(1):5–12, DOI [10.1023/A:1008196717017](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008196717017).** MIT Sloan MBA students in sealed-bid auctions for sports tickets and other items, randomised to pay by cash or card. The credit-card condition gave **significantly higher values for all three prizes**, and the premium was largest for Celtics tickets: average bids of **$61 vs $29** per MIT course slides, i.e. "over 100%" more. Limitations: small, elite student sample; high-variance auction bids; see the failed replication below. — [MIT OCW slides](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-821-listening-to-the-customer-fall-2002/5cc52570803b6e1bbea64585acdce779_redblack20copy.pdf); [MIT Spectrum 1999](https://betterworld.mit.edu/spectrum/issues/winter-1999/the-psychology-of-spending) [OTHER][S]
- **Thomas, Desai & Seenivasan (2011), "How Credit Card Payments Increase Unhealthy Food Purchases: Visceral Regulation of Vices", *Journal of Consumer Research* 38(1):126–139, DOI [10.1086/657331](https://doi.org/10.1086/657331).** Study 1: scanner data on **1,000 US households over 6 months**. Baskets held a larger share of items rated impulsive or unhealthy when paid by card. Studies 2–4 (experiments) show the effect is mediated by pain of payment and moderated by chronic sensitivity to it. Important for Norway: **debit cards were pooled with credit cards**, so this is a "card vs cash" result, not "credit vs debit". — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-657331.html); [ScienceDaily 2010](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/10/101018174345.htm) [OTHER][S]
- **Soman (2001), "Effects of Payment Mechanism on Spending Behavior: The Role of Rehearsal and Immediacy of Payments", *JCR* 27(4).** Payment forms that require less "rehearsal" (writing the amount, seeing money leave) and delay the outflow weaken memory of past spending and increase subsequent spending. — DOI not verified; [BK] (search: Soman 2001 JCR) [OTHER][BK]
- **Liu & Dewitte (2021), failed replication**, *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* 60 (2021). Four studies, **total n = 692**: **no credit card effect** on WTP or basket value, and no extension to mobile payments. The authors suggest the original effect may be inflated or fading. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joreco/v60y2021ics0969698921000382.html); [EMAC 2019 version](https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/A2019-4940.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Schomburgk, Belli & Hoffmann (2024), "Less cash, more splash? A meta-analysis on the cashless effect", *Journal of Retailing* 100(3):382–403.** Secondary coverage reports about **71 studies, 17 countries, ~11,000 participants**. There is a **small but significant cashless effect**; the credit-card effect **has weakened over time** and **depends on where data were collected**. Features of the cashless method do not moderate it; consumption situation and context do. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v100y2024i3p382-403.html); [CashEssentials summary](https://cashessentials.org/publication/less-cash-more-splash-a-meta-analysis-on-the-cashless-effect/) [OTHER][S]
- **Stanford GSB mega-replication, "A mega-replication of the effect of cash versus card payment on the pain of paying".** **13 preregistered replications, N = 32,371, 65 products, 57 price points ($1.09–$400).** Cash still feels more painful than cards. The **direct effect on amount spent is more reliable and larger than the effect on WTP**; the effect runs through pain of paying and is moderated by individual differences. Only the abstract was seen. — [Stanford GSB page](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/mega-replication-effect-cash-versus-card-payment-pain-paying) [OTHER][S]
- **De Nederlandsche Bank Working Paper 582 (2018), "Effects of payment instruments on unhealthy purchases".** Dutch online replication, **2,213 participants**. Card payers bought **12.3% more unhealthy supermarket products** than cash payers, but **pain-of-paying scores did not differ**, which casts doubt on the proposed mechanism. — [DNB WP 582 PDF](https://www.dnb.nl/media/3ozn3rds/working-paper-no-582_tcm47-371198.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Norwegian evidence on decoupling (Huse 2019, NHH).** Using Norwegian bank card data for 2008–2011, the thesis finds that **payment decoupling raises delinquency risk when reduced awareness of past spending encourages more borrowing**, and that persistent poor financial decision-making is also linked to higher risk. — [NHH Open Access](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2640771); [press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1109275) [NO][S]
- **Norwegian evidence on debt-type mental accounting: SIFO's "forbrukslån-paradoks" (Poppe; SIFO-rapport 13-2019, *Lånefinansiert forbruk i Norge anno 2019*).** Population survey. **32% of adults aged 18–80 had some unsecured debt** (overdraft, consumer loan or card debt). Many choose **more expensive card credit over a cheaper instalment consumer loan**. Respondents believed consumer loans were **more expensive and harder to control** than cards, and consumer loans have the **lowest status/legitimacy** despite being the cheapest unsecured option. Poppe attributes this to perceived flexibility, card credit feeling "safe", moral attitudes (borrowing is more acceptable the better the "investment"), and low knowledge of what each product involves. Those combining consumer loans and card debt are most exposed. — [OsloMet: "Stor forbrukslån-skam i Norge"](https://www.oslomet.no/forskning/forskningsnyheter/stor-forbrukslan-skam-i-norge); [ScienceNorway: "People are ashamed of their consumer loans"](https://www.sciencenorway.no/consumer-spending-debit-cards-economy/people-are-ashamed-of-their-consumer-loans/1929846) [NO][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability:**
  - Debt-type mental accounting (card credit is seen as "flexible and controllable", consumer loans as shameful): **VERIFIED, medium confidence**. It rests on a 2019 survey and a predictive thesis, not a causal design, and pre-dates the 2022–24 rate rise.
  - Decoupling leading to over-borrowing and delinquency: **VERIFIED (weak), medium-low confidence** (one bank, 2008–2011).
  - Credit card versus debit spending premium: **HYPOTHESIS, low confidence**. Norway is close to cashless, so the cash comparison that drives most of the literature is weak there. The meta-analysis shows the effect fading and varying by location, and Thomas et al. pooled debit with credit.
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Separate the card's two jobs clearly: "payment tool (free if you pay in full)" versus "loan (one of the most expensive ways to borrow)".
  - Counter the forbrukslån paradox neutrally. Show that revolving card credit typically costs more than an instalment loan or refinancing at the same balance, without encouraging new debt.
  - Recommend recoupling features: monthly spend summaries, real-time notifications, a self-set limit.
  - Do not describe "pay later" or the interest-free period as "free money".

### Gaps
- Norges Bank payment-habit survey data on credit versus debit use at the point of sale could not be fetched.
- No Norwegian experiment on credit versus debit spending was found.
- The SIFO 2019 shame/status percentages were not retrieved (only qualitative summary).

## 3. Minimum-payment anchoring and other repayment heuristics

### Takeaway
Showing a minimum payment pulls repayments down for a meaningful minority. Estimates: 9–22% of accounts in US data, and default effects equal to about 8% of all interest paid in UK data. "Fixes" through disclosure or active choice change behaviour at the margin, but evidence they reduce debt is weak. People also misallocate repayments across cards (balance-matching). No Norwegian study was found. **Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium confidence.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** Anchoring is an arbitrary number shown at the moment of decision pulling judgments toward it. For cards, the minimum payment on the statement or app acts as an anchor for people who pay more than the minimum but less than in full. — [Stewart 2009, Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/2552) [OTHER][S]
- **Stewart (2009), "The Cost of Anchoring on Credit-Card Minimum Repayments", *Psychological Science* 20(1):39–41, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02255.x.** (1) Survey of **248 real UK bills**: among partial payers, the payment chosen correlated with the stated minimum; full payers paid in full whatever the minimum. (2) Experiment with **~413 UK volunteers** (press figure) and a mock bill of **£435.76**: participants given **no minimum repaid on average £175 (≈40% of the balance)**, more than those shown a minimum. Simulations suggest the minimum could **double lifetime interest**, though Stewart notes the effect could be much larger or smaller depending on debt and repayments. Proposed remedy: show what alternative repayments cost over time. Limitations: hypothetical bill; online volunteers. — [WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/2552); [ScienceDaily 2008](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/10/081006130542.htm); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-psychological-bias-leads-many-people-pay-more-credit-card-interest) [OTHER][S]
- **Navarro-Martinez, Salisbury, Lemon, Stewart, Matthews & Harris (2011), "Minimum Required Payment and Supplemental Information Disclosure Effects on Consumer Debt Repayment Decisions", *Journal of Marketing Research* 48 (Special Issue):S60–S77**, DOI [10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S60](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S60). Experiments confirming the minimum-payment anchor and testing supplementary disclosures. Exact effect sizes were not verified this session. — [BK] [OTHER]
- **Keys & Wang (2019), "Minimum Payments and Debt Paydown in Consumer Credit Cards", *JFE* 131(3):528–548.** Data covering about a quarter of the US general-purpose card market. **29% of accounts regularly pay at or near the minimum.** Using issuers' changes to minimum-payment formulas to separate anchoring from liquidity constraints, **at least 22% of near-minimum payers (9% of all accounts)** respond in ways consistent with anchoring (an earlier draft said 9–20% of all accounts). CARD Act disclosures led to **fewer than 1% of accounts adopting the suggested alternative payment**, about **$62m/year** in interest savings, versus **>$2bn/year** if all anchored consumers had adopted it. — [RePEc JFE record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:131:y:2019:i:3:p:528-548); [NBER w22742](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22742) [OTHER][S]
- **Sakaguchi, Stewart, Gathergood, Adams, Guttman-Kenney, Hayes & Hunt (2022), "Default Effects of Credit Card Minimum Payments", *JMR* 59(4):775–796, DOI [10.1177/00222437211070589](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437211070589)** (open access, CC BY). UK card transaction data. Once people set **automatic minimum payment** as their default, they stop making the occasional larger payments they used to make. The authors estimate this accounts for **about 8% of all interest ever paid** (an earlier version said 12%). **Manual payments also fall when minimum-payment information is shown.** Recommended remedy: **shroud the minimum-payment option** and direct attention to the full balance. — [WRAP open copy](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/160903/); [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2023/03/01/how-default-minimum-payments-lead-to-more-credit-card-debt/) [OTHER][S]
- **Guttman-Kenney, Adams, Hunt, Laibson & Stewart (2025), "The Semblance of Success in Nudging Consumers to Pay Down Credit Card Debt", *AEJ: Economic Policy* 17(4):72–105** (earlier versions: FCA Occasional Paper 45, July 2018; NBER w31926, Dec 2023). UK survey experiment plus field RCT using an **active-choice** nudge that removes the default minimum. Enrolment in minimum-payment autopay fell from **36.9% to 9.6%**, and after six months the share paying **exactly the minimum fell 23%**. But **credit card debt did not fall after seven cycles**, because nudged cardholders picked autopay amounts only slightly above the minimum (liquidity limits). This is the key counter-evidence that de-anchoring alone does not reduce debt. — [LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/130080); [NBER w31926 PDF](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31926/w31926.pdf); [FCA OP45](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-45.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Medina (2021), "Side Effects of Nudging: Evidence from a Randomized Intervention in the Credit Card Market", *Review of Financial Studies* 34(5):2580–2607.** Brazil; field RCT on a financial-management platform. Payment reminders **cut card late fees by 14%** but **raised checking-account overdraft fees by 9%**, concentrated among prior overdraft users. Those users had a **net 5% rise in total fees**, while the rest saved 15%. — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:34:y:2021:i:5:p:2580-2607.); [AEA RCT registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/3034) [OTHER][S]
- **Gathergood, Mahoney, Stewart & Weber (2019), "How Do Individuals Repay Their Debt? The Balance-Matching Heuristic", *American Economic Review* 109(3):844–875, DOI [10.1257/aer.20180288](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180288).** Linked UK card data for **1.4 million individuals over two years**. Cost-minimisation implies two-card holders should put **97.1%** of excess payments on the higher-rate card; they actually allocate **51.5%**. Misallocation does not vary with the stakes, which argues against optimisation frictions. Repayments instead follow **balance matching** (each card's payment share mirrors its balance share), which captures over half the predictable variation and is persistent within individuals. — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20180288); [Nottingham repository](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/54171) [OTHER][S]
- **Debt account aversion.** People prefer to close small debt accounts first rather than pay down the highest-interest debt. — [Amar, Ariely, Ayal, Cryder & Rick 2011, "Winning the Battle but Losing the War: The Psychology of Debt Management", *JMR* 48(SPL):S38–S50, DOI 10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S38](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S38) [OTHER][BK]
- **UK regulatory response (FCA Credit Card Market Study, MS14/6, final findings July 2016).** The FCA analysed **34 million consumers' card accounts over five years** and surveyed **~40,000 consumers**. **650,000** people had been in persistent debt for **three years or more**, and a further **750,000** had made **systematic minimum repayments** over that period. Firms lacked strong incentives to help. From **1 September 2018** firms must identify customers in persistent debt, prompt them, offer repayment plans where prompts fail, and intervene earlier. The FCA expected this to save **3+ million customers up to £1.3bn/year**. — [FCA market study page](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/market-studies/credit-card-market-study); [final findings PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/market-studies/ms14-6-3-credit-card-market-study-final-findings-report.pdf); [Mondo Visione summary](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/uks-financial-conduct-authority-publishes-final-findings-of-credit-card-market-2016726/) [OTHER][S]
- **Nordic anchoring on regulatory numbers.** After Sweden's 1 September 2018 cap on high-cost short-term credit (nominal rate ≤ reference rate + 40 pp; total costs ≤ loan amount), Finansinspektionen found the cap had a **"normative effect"**: **over 96% of new high-cost loans in 2019 carried nominal rates between 38% and 39.5%**, just under the cap. Lenders converged on the regulatory number. — [FI-analys 22 (English)](https://fi.se/contentassets/b9c6954789a44fa6ab8e0f48faceae47/fi-analys-22-hogkostnadskrediter-engn.pdf) [NORDIC][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium confidence.** Anchoring is a robust cognitive effect, and Norwegian card statements and apps very likely display a minimum payment (HYPOTHESIS; Norwegian minimum-payment rules and display practice were not verified). The relevant group is the share of Norwegian cardholders who revolve. Kredinor estimates interest-bearing balances at about half of card balances in 2025 (commercial source, §12). Balance-matching is INFERRED (medium) for Norwegians who hold several cards alongside a consumer loan, the high-risk combination SIFO identifies (§2).
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Never headline "lav minstebetaling" (low minimum payment) as a benefit.
  - In calculators, **default to full payment**; defaults matter (see ESRI, §11).
  - Show a repayment table: time to clear and total interest at the minimum, at a fixed NOK amount and at full payment. This is the remedy Stewart proposed.
  - For multi-debt users, show "pay the highest-rate debt first" explicitly, against balance-matching.
  - Be honest that nudges have limited effects for cash-constrained users; for them, signpost free debt advice rather than optimisation tips.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian or Nordic study of minimum-payment anchoring on cards was found.
- Norwegian minimum-payment conventions (e.g., % of balance or NOK floor) were not verified.
- No CFPB credit card disclosure research was retrieved this session.

## 4. Teaser rates, shrouded attributes and overconfidence about future borrowing

### Takeaway
Card pricing is "back-loaded": low headline or introductory prices and rewards, with hidden or contingent fees and high post-promotion rates. This pays off because consumers underestimate their future borrowing and fees. Regulation that caps or unshrouds fees (CARD Act) cut costs without obvious offsets. Nordic regulators have capped prices and restricted marketing, and Norway requires representative examples. No Norwegian study tests consumer reactions to teasers. **Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium confidence.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definition: shrouded attributes.** Add-on prices (fees, penalty rates) that myopic consumers do not anticipate. In competitive equilibrium firms may not unshroud them, because doing so educates consumers to avoid add-ons without winning their custom. — [Gabaix & Laibson 2006, "Shrouded Attributes, Consumer Myopia, and Information Suppression in Competitive Markets", *QJE* 121(2):505–540, DOI 10.1162/qjec.2006.121.2.505](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2006.121.2.505) [OTHER][BK]
- **Definition and canonical evidence: overconfidence about future borrowing.** US card interest rates stayed high and sticky in the 1980s despite falling costs of funds. Ausubel's explanation is that many consumers **do not expect to borrow**, so they ignore the rate when choosing a card. — Ausubel 1991, "The Failure of Competition in the Credit Card Market", *AER* 81(1):50–81 (no DOI; JSTOR) [OTHER][BK]. Supporting experimental evidence: consumers prefer short, low teasers that are worse ex post (Shui & Ausubel, §1). — [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/b3bc4c71-9c90-4dfe-9ddb-dbfd108a0296) [OTHER][S]
- **Ru & Schoar, "Do Credit Card Companies Screen for Behavioral Biases?" (NBER WP 22360, 2016; revised as BIS WP 842, 2020).** About **one million pre-approved card offers mailed to US households, 1999–2011** (Compremedia). Issuers target **less-educated** customers with more back-loaded terms: **higher late, over-limit and penalty fees** together with **more up-front inducements** (low intro APRs, cash back, annual-fee waivers) and more shrouded designs. Using state unemployment-insurance changes as variation in credit risk, issuers rely more on back-loaded fees when customers are less exposed to cash-flow shocks. The authors note that behavioural and preference-based explanations are hard to separate. — [NBER digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/sep16/w22360.html); [BIS WP 842](https://www.bis.org/publ/work842.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Agarwal, Chomsisengphet, Mahoney & Stroebel (2015), "Regulating Consumer Financial Products: Evidence from Credit Cards", *QJE* 130(1):111–164** (DOI 10.1093/qje/qju037 [BK]). Difference-in-differences comparing US consumer and small-business cards around the 2009 CARD Act. In the published version, fee limits **reduced overall borrowing costs by an annualised 1.6% of average daily balances**, and by **more than 5.3% for FICO < 660**. Headline figures changed across drafts (2.8%, $11.9bn, $12.6bn and $20.8bn/year all appear in different versions), so cite the QJE version. [BK] The published abstract reports no evidence of offsetting increases in interest charges or reduced credit volume. — [RePEc QJE record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:1:p:111-164); [NBER w19484](https://www.nber.org/papers/19484); [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/index%2ephp/digest/feb14/new-evidence-effects-credit-card-regulations) [OTHER][S]
- **Norwegian marketing rules on credit (context, not behavioural evidence).** Forbrukertilsynet publishes guidance on the regulation on marketing of credit (*veiledning til forskrift om markedsføring av kreditt*, §§ 4–5, June 2020), referencing finansavtaleloven. — [Forbrukertilsynet guidance PDF](https://forbrukertilsynet.no/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/veiledning-til-forskrift-om-markedsforing-av-kreditt.pdf) [NO][S]. Industry compliance guidance says terms like "rentefritt" (interest-free) or "10% rente" trigger the duty to give a **representative example** (effective rate, total cost, credit amount, term, total repayable). — [Resurs Bank co-marketing guidelines (NO)](https://www.resursbank.no/api/file-download/uploads/sites/2/2025/12/19135552/Co-marketing-Guidelines_NO_.pdf); [Svea "Markedsføring av kreditt"](https://www.svea.com/globalassets/norway/about-svea-ekonomi/personvern/markedsf%C3%B8ring-av-kreditt.pdf) [NO][S, industry documents]
- **Norway brought consumer debt into its lending regulation in 2019**, and volumes fell for several years afterwards. — [Norges Bank, Bankplassen 2026](https://www.norges-bank.no/bankplassen/arkiv/2026/dyrtid-og-hoye-renter-har-ikke-fort-til-storre-forbrukslan/) [NO][S]
- **Nordic price regulation and firm adaptation.**
  - Denmark: a **35% ÅOP cap** (plus a 100% total-cost cap) on consumer loans from **1 July 2020**, with marketing restrictions for loans above 25% ÅOP. Before that, KFST estimated that in 2014 consumers paid about **DKK 140m to borrow ~DKK 436m** in short "kviklån" averaging 30 days, typically at about **700% ÅOP**. — [Dansk Erhverv](https://www.danskerhverv.dk/presse-og-nyheder/nyheder/lov-om-aop-loft-pa-35-pct.-pa-forbrugslan-pa-vej/); [KFST kviklån report 2015](https://en.kfst.dk/media/2779/20150701-markedet-for-kviklaan-opdateret-juli-2015.pdf) [NORDIC][S]
  - Finland: a 2013 cap on loans under €2,000 was **circumvented by offering larger loans and limits**. A 20% cap followed in September 2019, and since October 2023 the cap is 15% plus the reference rate (max 20%). — [Bank of Finland Bulletin 2019](https://www.bofbulletin.fi/en/2019/2/new-methods-needed-to-rein-in-consumer-credit/); [Finnish Legal Services Authority](https://www.oikeuspalveluvirasto.fi/en/financial-and-debt-counselling/restructuring-debts/payday-loans/) [NORDIC][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium confidence.** The consumer-side mechanism (underestimating future borrowing; not attending to contingent fees) is cognitive, and there is no reason to expect Norwegians to be immune. Norwegian rules (effective rate, representative example, the 2019 lending regulation, the debt register) probably reduce the most extreme shrouding. Norwegian card marketing commonly stresses an interest-free period, bonuses and insurance (HYPOTHESIS; market practice not systematically verified this session).
- Finnish circumvention and Swedish clustering under the cap show that **firms adapt their pricing to whatever metric is regulated or compared**. A comparison service's ranking metric will become a target in the same way (INFERRED).
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Rank by **expected 12-month cost for the user's stated usage**: share of months revolving, foreign spending, cash withdrawals. Do not rank by headline rate or teaser.
  - Always show the post-interest-free-period effective rate and the add-on fees (currency mark-up, cash-withdrawal fee, paper-invoice fee, annual fee) on the card tile, not behind a click.
  - HYPOTHESIS (legal check needed): a comparison service promoting credit is likely within the scope of Norwegian credit-marketing rules, so representative examples should appear wherever "rentefritt" or rates are mentioned. Verify against Forbrukertilsynet's guidance.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian study of teaser or interest-free-period effects on card choice was found.
- Ausubel's 1999 "Adverse selection in the credit card market" evidence (stronger response to teaser than post-teaser rates) was not verified.
- The FCA CCMS findings on balance-transfer and promotional customers were not retrieved.

## 5. Rewards, cashback and benefits: effects on spending and card choice

### Takeaway
Cashback raises card spending and debt, most strongly among liquidity-constrained and less financially literate consumers. In the US, rewards redistribute money from naive to sophisticated cardholders (≈$15bn/yr) and from cash/debit users to card users. No Norwegian or Nordic evidence was found. **Norway applicability: spending response INFERRED (medium-low); redistribution magnitude HYPOTHESIS (low)**, because the European interchange-fee regime is different.

### Cited Findings
- **Agarwal, Chakravorti & Lunn (2010), "Why Do Banks Reward their Customers to Use their Credit Cards?", Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago WP 2010-19** (published version titled "Cash-back rewards: Effects on spending and debt accumulation"). Data from a large US bank; panel/event-study around enrolment in a cash-back programme. Working-paper estimate: an average **$25 cash-back reward** raised spending by **$79/month** and debt by **$191/month** in the first quarter. The published abstract reports **spending +32% and debt +8%, persisting long-run**. Some spending is **substituted from other cards** (credit-bureau data). Responses are larger for **more liquidity-constrained and less financially literate** consumers. Limitation: one issuer and self-selected enrolment. The magnitudes differ between versions. — [RePEc WP 2010-19](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/fipfedhwp/wp-2010-19.htm); [Lingnan University record (published version)](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/cash-back-rewards-effects-on-spending-and-debt-accumulation/) [OTHER][S]
- **Agarwal, Presbitero, Silva & Wix (2022/2023), "Who Pays For Your Rewards? Redistribution in the Credit Card Market"** (CEPR DP17733; Fed FEDS 2023; IMF WP 2023/054). US supervisory account-level data; "net reward" = rewards minus interest and fees. **Regardless of income, sophisticated cardholders profit from reward cards at the expense of naive ones.** Banks push reward cards by pricing them below comparable non-reward cards. Preliminary slides put annual redistribution at about **$15bn**. The sub-figures quoted in snippets ($4.1bn paid and $1.3bn earned) do not sum obviously to this, so use the headline with caution. Industry commentators argue credit score, not income, is the relevant correlate. — [Federal Reserve FEDS page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/who-pays-for-your-rewards-redistribution-in-the-credit-card-market.htm); [CEPR DP17733](https://voxeu.org/publications/dp17733); [Philadelphia Fed slides](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/events/2022/consumer-finance/presbitero-presentation-102622.pdf); counter-view: [View from the Wing](https://viewfromthewing.com/credit-card-rewards-arent-a-tax-on-the-poor-heres-who-actually-pays-for-your-points/) [OTHER][S]
- **Egan et al., "Who Pays for Payments?" (NBER w35067).** US; interchange fees transfer about **$30bn per year from cash and debit users to credit card users**. — [NBER w35067](https://www.nber.org/index%2ephp/papers/w35067) [OTHER][S]
- **Ru & Schoar (above): rewards and cash-back are among the up-front inducements** offered disproportionately to less-educated households alongside back-loaded fees. — [NBER digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/sep16/w22360.html) [OTHER][S]
- **Ching & Hayashi (2010), "Payment card rewards programs and consumer payment choice", *Journal of Banking & Finance* 34(8)** (DOI 10.1016/j.jbankfin.2010.02.015 [BK]). US survey data. Rewards shift payment-instrument choice toward the rewarded card. Magnitudes not verified this session. [OTHER][BK]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: spending/debt response INFERRED, medium-low confidence.** The behavioural channel (rewards make card use feel like gaining, and encourage concentrating spending on one card) is general. But: (a) Norwegian cards tend to emphasise travel insurance, bonus or discount programmes rather than US-style cashback (HYPOTHESIS, not verified); (b) interchange caps in the EEA probably limit reward funding. That the EU Interchange Fee Regulation applies in Norway is **not verified this session**. So the size of US-style cross-subsidies is a **HYPOTHESIS** for Norway.
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Show **net value** = expected rewards/benefits minus expected interest and fees for the user's profile.
  - Include an explicit rule of thumb: "Bonus and benefits only pay off if you pay the full bill every month."
  - Value insurance benefits realistically, e.g. flag duplication with existing home or travel insurance (HYPOTHESIS on prevalence).
  - Avoid "earn more by spending more" framing; it can raise spending, especially among liquidity-constrained users.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian or Nordic study of reward or benefit effects on card spending or choice was found.
- Norwegian interchange regulation and its effect on rewards were not verified.
- Schuh, Shy & Stavins (2010, Boston Fed) on cross-subsidies was not retrieved.

## 6. Choice overload and decision avoidance in financial products

### Takeaway
The "too many options" effect is **not a general law**. Meta-analyses find a mean effect near zero, but substantial effects when choice sets are complex, tasks difficult, preferences uncertain and the goal is to minimise effort. Those conditions describe credit card choice well. With complex comparisons, consumers lean on rank position and reviews. **Norway applicability: HYPOTHESIS/INFERRED, low-medium confidence.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** Choice overload: larger or more complex assortments lower choice probability, satisfaction or confidence, or raise deferral (decision avoidance) and regret. — [Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015, Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual) [OTHER][S]
- **Canonical study.** In the "jam" field experiment, a 24-jam display attracted more shoppers but produced far fewer purchases (about 3%) than a 6-jam display (about 30%). — [Iyengar & Lepper 2000, *JPSP* 79(6):995–1006, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995) [OTHER][BK]
- **Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload", *JCR* 37(3).** **63 conditions from 50 experiments, N = 5,036**: **mean effect size virtually zero**, with considerable variance between studies. — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/can-there-ever-be-too-many-options-a-meta-analytic-review-of-choice-overload-scheibehenne-benjamin/10008756224) [OTHER][S]
- **Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), "Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis", *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 25(2):333–358** (DOI 10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002 [BK]). **99 observations, N = 7,202.** Four moderators reliably increase overload: **choice-set complexity, decision-task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and an effort-minimising goal**. Satisfaction/confidence, regret, deferral and switching likelihood work equally well as outcome measures. The pooled effect size was not verified. One secondary blog cites d ≈ 0.5–0.6 when all moderators are high; that figure is unverified. — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual/) [OTHER][S]
- **McShane & Böckenholt (2018) reanalysis.** At low moderator levels the effect **reverses for some outcomes and disappears for others**. — [Scheibehenne reply PDF (context)](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.reply.2011.pdf); [search summary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800528) [OTHER][S]
- **Comparison-tool behaviour.** The European Commission's comparison-tools study found in behavioural experiments that **link position highly affects consumers' choice** and that user reviews and ratings clearly influence it. In complex decisions consumers delegate to rank order. — [European Commission study page](https://commission.europa.eu/publications/comparison-tools-and-third-party-verification-schemes_en); [London Economics](https://londoneconomics.co.uk/blog/publication/study-on-the-coverage-functioning-and-consumer-use-of-comparison-tools-and-third-party-verification-schemes-for-such-tools-march-2015) [OTHER (EU-wide)][S]
- **Credit-product confusion.** In Irish PRICE Lab experiments, consumers found personal loans confusing and struggled to pick the right one. — [ESRI "Consumers Are Confused by Personal Loans"](https://www.esri.ie/node/6289); [ESRI report BKMNEXT314](https://www.esri.ie/system/files/publications/BKMNEXT314%20%281%29.pdf) [OTHER][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: HYPOTHESIS/INFERRED, low-medium confidence.** Choosing a credit card involves many attributes (rate, interest-free days, fees, insurance, bonus, limit) and uncertain preferences, since people do not know whether they will revolve. Those are the high-moderator conditions in Chernev et al. No Norwegian test exists.
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Simplify the *task*, not just the number of cards. Ask 2–3 profiling questions (pay in full or not, travel or foreign spend, need for a buffer), then show a shortlist of about 3–5 cards with one comparable cost metric, with "show all" available.
  - Because rank position drives choice, the **ranking method is the product**. Make it transparent and needs-based (see §9).

### Gaps
- No financial-product-specific choice-overload study for credit cards was found.
- Iyengar, Huberman & Jiang's 401(k) participation result (fewer enrolments with more funds) was not verified this session.

## 7. Status quo bias and switching inertia (credit cards and banking)

### Takeaway
UK and US evidence shows strong inertia in current accounts (about 3% switching per year in the UK) and reluctance to switch cards (structural switching cost ≈ $150). The FCA found card customers do shop around and switch, but mainly over promotional offers; competition on standard rates and fees is weak. Norway's consumer surveys show about one in three people switch or renegotiate *some* bank service each year, but give no card-specific figure. **Norway applicability: banking-level switching VERIFIED (descriptive); card-specific inertia INFERRED, medium confidence.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** Status quo bias / switching inertia means staying with the current provider despite better alternatives, because of switching costs (real or perceived), attention costs, or a lack of trigger points. — [CMA retail banking summary of final report, 9 Aug 2016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a8c0fb40f0b608a7000002/summary-of-final-report-retail-banking-investigation.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **UK CMA Retail Banking Market Investigation (final report, 9 August 2016).** About **3% of personal current-account customers (4% of SMEs) switch per year**; this figure comes from secondary sources and was not found in the primary text. The CMA identified complex charging structures, **a lack of trigger points**, and **low awareness of the Current Account Switch Service**, as well as fear that switching is difficult or might go wrong. Remedies included **prompts to review arrangements** and Open Banking. — [CMA full final report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57ac9667e5274a0f6c00007a/retail-banking-market-investigation-full-final-report.pdf); [Economics Online summary](https://economicsonline.co.uk/Business_economics/CMA_retail_banking_investigation.html); [choose.co.uk](https://www.choose.co.uk/news/cma-banks-current-accounts-energy-tariffs-competition-transparency-regulation.html) [OTHER][S]
- **UK FCA Credit Card Market Study (2016).** Consumers **shop around, switch and value flexibility**, and competition works reasonably well for most. But there is **less competitive pressure on interest rates outside promotional offers and on other fees**, and **higher-risk consumers have fewer options and find shopping around harder**. — [FCA market study](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/market-studies/credit-card-market-study) [OTHER][S]
- **Shui & Ausubel: cardholders are very reluctant to switch**, and even past switchers often fail to switch again; the structural average switching cost is ≈ **$150**. — [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/b3bc4c71-9c90-4dfe-9ddb-dbfd108a0296) [OTHER][S]
- **Norway: Forbrukerrådet's annual switching survey (Bytteundersøkelsen, Ipsos, each January).** **32%** switched or renegotiated bank services in 2023 (**25%** in 2022), **34%** in 2024 and **33%** in 2025. Counting bank *or* insurance, **45%** switched or renegotiated at least one service in 2024 and **44%** in 2025. No credit-card-specific share was found in the snippets. This measure **includes renegotiation**, so it is not comparable with the UK's 3% current-account switching rate. — [Bytteundersøkelsen 2024](https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2024/06/rapport-bytteundersokelsen-2024.pdf); [2025](https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2025/05/bytteundersokelsen-2025.pdf); [2026](https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2026/05/bytteundersokelsen-2026-rapport.pdf) [NO][S]
- **Norway: active debt substitution.** Using the debt register linked to tax data for all households, 2020–2024, households **substitute unsecured debt with other debt (e.g., mortgages) around house purchase**; housing gives access to cheaper credit through refinancing. — [Norges Bank Staff Memo 8/2024 (Gautam, Getz Wold, Gulbrandsen & Nenov)](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/publications/Staff-Memo/2024/staff-memo-8-2024-household-accumulation-of-consumer-debt/) [NO][S]. An NHH master's thesis (2019) finds results consistent with borrowers substituting low-cost mortgages with high-cost consumer credit after mortgage tightening. — [NHH Open Access](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2611623) [NO][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: VERIFIED that a substantial minority actively switch or renegotiate bank products (descriptive survey); card-specific inertia INFERRED, medium confidence.** Cards with no annual fee and an unused limit create little pain from staying, so attention-based inertia is plausible. The Gjeldsregisteret creates a Norway-specific *reason to act*: unused card limits are registered credit lines that lenders see. HYPOTHESIS: this is a trigger to cancel or consolidate cards before a mortgage application; verify with Gjeldsregisteret or Finanstilsynet sources. Commercial guides on deleting cards from the register exist, e.g. [kredittkortlisten.no](https://www.kredittkortlisten.no/guider/hvordan-slette-kredittkort-i-gjeldsregisteret/) [NO][S, commercial].
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Build around **trigger points**: rate increases, the end of an interest-free or promotional period, annual-fee renewal, mortgage application, a move or life event.
  - Quantify savings in **NOK per year**.
  - Lower perceived switching costs with step-by-step guidance (apply, move autopay and subscriptions, cancel the old card, update the debt register).
  - Do not overpromise savings for higher-risk users, who have fewer options (FCA).

### Gaps
- No Norwegian credit-card switching rate was found. The full Bytteundersøkelsen PDFs may include one; they were not fetched.
- No evidence on balance-transfer switching in Norway (balance-transfer products appear uncommon there; HYPOTHESIS).
- The FCA CCMS balance-transfer statistics were not retrieved.

## 8. Loss aversion, framing of costs, APR misunderstanding and exponential-growth bias

### Takeaway
Consumers systematically underestimate interest costs (exponential-growth bias) and use APR poorly ("low APR = low total cost"). Showing costs in **currency amounts** changes behaviour more than APR alone: US payday borrowing fell 11–23% with dollar-cost disclosure, and adding total amount repayable improves comparison. Loss framing specific to credit is thinly evidenced. **Norway applicability: EGB and APR misunderstanding INFERRED (medium-high); loss-frame effects HYPOTHESIS.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions.** *Exponential-growth bias (EGB)*: the tendency to linearise compound growth, which leads people to underestimate the interest rate implied by a payment stream and the future value of compounding balances. *Loss aversion*: losses weigh more than equal gains. *Framing*: equivalent information presented differently changes choices. — [Stango & Zinman 2009, *Journal of Finance* 64(6):2807–2849, RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:64:y:2009:i:6:p:2807-2849) (DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01518.x [BK]) [OTHER][S]
- **Stango & Zinman (2009), "Exponential Growth Bias and Household Finance".** US household survey data. EGB explains the tendency to **underestimate an interest rate given other loan terms**. **More-biased households borrow more, save less, favour shorter maturities**, and use and benefit more from advice, conditional on rich controls. EGB is not just a proxy for financial sophistication. In the companion paper ("Fuzzy Math and Red Ink"), a payment stream implying **50% APR** is underestimated by **~30 percentage points** by the typical consumer. Limitation: correlational. — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:64:y:2009:i:6:p:2807-2849); [NBER SI 2008 draft](https://img.nber.org/conferences/2008/si2008/LE/stango.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Stango & Zinman (2011), "Fuzzy Math, Disclosure Regulation, and Market Outcomes" (RFS).** When Truth-in-Lending APR disclosure was **enforced**, the gap between rates paid by the most and least biased borrowers **compressed**. Standardised APR disclosure helps biased consumers. — [summary via search; LSE Levy-Tasoff paper context](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/levymr/papers/LevyTasoff.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Bertrand & Morse (2011), "Information Disclosure, Cognitive Biases, and Payday Borrowing", *Journal of Finance*** (DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01698.x [BK]). Field RCT with **1,441 borrowers at 77 US payday stores, 2008–09**. Showing the **accumulated dollar cost of fees** cut borrowing per pay cycle by **$55 (−23%)**, against −16% for APR information and −12% for refinancing-frequency information. Take-up of future loans fell by **11% over four months**. Texas later mandated a similar disclosure (2012). An NBER evaluation reports a **5.9 pp drop** in likelihood of borrowing. — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/information-disclosure-cognitive-biases-and-payday-borrowing-united-states); [NBER w28765 (Texas)](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28765/w28765.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **FCA Research Note (29 April 2026), "Navigating the disclosure trade-off: balancing flexibility and standardisation in cost of credit information"** (Sheth, Hayes, Keeley, Li & Spang). Online experiment in which participants compared pairs of credit products under different cost disclosures. **APR was poorly understood yet still influential**, and many used a **"low APR = low total cost" rule of thumb**. **Giving APR for one product but not another impaired comparability**; **adding the total amount repayable to APR improved comparability**. The work fed into CP26/15; the FCA is not proposing rule changes yet. — [FCA research note](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/research-note-navigating-disclosure-trade-balancing-flexibility-and-standardisation-cost-credit); [PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/navigating-disclosure-trade-off-balancing-flexibility-standardisation-cost-credit-information.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **Lunn, Bohacek & Rybicki (2016), "An Experimental Investigation of Personal Loan Choices", ESRI Research Series.** Irish lab experiment. When **total cost** information was shown, consumers **chose shorter loan terms**; choices depended on whether APR and total cost were shown alongside duration and monthly repayment. — [ESRI report](https://www.esri.ie/system/files/publications/BKMNEXT314%20%281%29.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/b/esr/resser/bkmnext314.html) [OTHER][S]
- **UK study in *Journal of Economic Psychology* 32(4):609–620 (2011), "Understanding and knowledge of credit cost and duration".** When given APR, participants **significantly overestimated the total cost** of a 12-month loan. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joepsy/v32y2011i4p609-620.html) [OTHER][S]
- **Norwegian financial-knowledge context.**
  - Nyhus (2023), *Finansiell kunnskap i den norske befolkningen*: women score lower than men on savings, loans and inflation, worst among women in their 20s; Norwegians nevertheless rank high internationally. — [ScienceNorway](https://www.sciencenorway.no/economy-finance-finans/women-with-higher-education-know-as-little-about-personal-finance-as-men-with-a-primary-school-education/2282113) [NO][S]
  - PISA 2022: Norwegian 15-year-olds have **"middels" financial competence, below the OECD average**, and rarely compare prices. — [Udir](https://udir.no/api/PdfApi/PrintPageAsPdfDocument/214861); [ScienceNorway](https://sciencenorway.no/economy-finance/how-much-do-15-year-olds-know-about-finances/2389216) [NO][S]
- **Norwegian card price level (commercial source).** Credit cards typically carry **over 25% effective rate, often close to 30%**. — [bytt.no (comparison site)](https://www.bytt.no/bank/forbrukslan/a-bep/skal-du-velge-et-forbrukslan-eller-et-kredittkort) [NO][S, commercial; verify against Finansportalen]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: EGB and APR misunderstanding INFERRED, medium-high confidence.** These are cognitive arithmetic biases replicated across countries. Norwegian financial-knowledge data show gaps (young women, adolescents) that make them plausible, but no Norwegian EGB/credit study was found. Norway mandates *effektiv rente* in marketing, so the FCA's "low APR = low cost" heuristic is directly relevant: Norwegian consumers see effective rates everywhere, and those rates hide the role of fees and credit size.
- **Loss framing: HYPOTHESIS, low-medium confidence.** No credit-specific loss-vs-gain framing experiment was found. Framing "you are losing NOK X per year" vs "save NOK X" should be A/B tested, not assumed.
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Lead with **NOK amounts**: "Carrying NOK 20,000 for 12 months costs about NOK X on this card vs NOK Y on that card", plus the total repayable. Keep effective rate as a secondary figure, consistent with the FCA finding that APR plus total repayable beats APR alone.
  - Use standard scenarios so every card is comparable on the same basis, and never show a rate for one card but not another.
  - Use visuals of compounding (balance-over-time charts) to counter EGB.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian experiment on currency-amount vs effective-rate framing was found.
- No credit-specific loss-aversion study was found.
- The detailed treatment effects of the FCA 2026 note were not retrieved.

## 9. Financial self-efficacy, trust and perceived credibility of information sources (including comparison sites)

### Takeaway
UK and EU evidence shows comparison-site users report high trust and satisfaction. Yet many do not understand commission models, distrust how their data are used, do not know how to complain, and follow rank order heavily. Regulators therefore push transparency on market coverage, business model and ranking. Norway has a public, mandatory-reporting comparison portal (Finansportalen) but no trust evaluation was found. **Norway applicability: INFERRED (trust heuristics), with Norway-specific trust levels a HYPOTHESIS; low-medium confidence.**

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** *Financial self-efficacy*: belief in one's ability to manage financial tasks. *Source credibility*: perceived expertise and trustworthiness of an information source, which shapes how far its rankings are relied on. No primary self-efficacy and credit card study was retrieved this session (see Gaps).
- **UK CMA Digital Comparison Tools market study (final report 26 Sept 2017; Kantar Public consumer research, March 2017).**
  - A survey of **over 4,000 consumers** found **overall trust and satisfaction among DCT users were high**, and users said DCTs helped them choose and save.
  - Consumers **did not trust DCTs to use personal data appropriately** and had **low awareness of how to complain**.
  - Remedies focused on transparency about **market coverage, business models and ranking methods**.
  - The Financial Services Consumer Panel noted consumers often **do not realise the firm receives commission** on click-through.
  - Exact survey percentages were not retrieved.
  — [CMA final report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/59c93546e5274a77468120d6/digital-comparison-tools-market-study-final-report.pdf); [Kantar consumer research final report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/58e224f5e5274a06b3000099/dcts-consumer-research-final-report.pdf); [Simmons & Simmons summary of preliminary findings](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/ck0ad2adin8d80b85tzw62x8u/180417-cma-publishes-preliminary-findings-of-digital-comparison-tools); [FSCP response](https://fca.org.uk/panels/consumer-panel/publication/fscp_response_cma_digital_comparison_tools.pdf) [OTHER][S]
- **European Commission (2014 study, published 2015; London Economics/ECME), "Study on the coverage, functioning and consumer use of comparison tools and third-party verification schemes".**
  - **74%** of EU consumers had used a comparison tool and **40%** use one at least monthly.
  - **65%** had experienced a problem: product unavailable in 54% of cases, incorrect prices in 21%.
  - **Fewer than half** of tools disclosed supplier relationships, business model or data sourcing. Only **34%** told users how to complain, and **11%** gave no contact details.
  - Third-party verification schemes were rare.
  - Link position and reviews strongly influenced choice.
  — [European Commission page](https://commission.europa.eu/publications/comparison-tools-and-third-party-verification-schemes_en); [Commission staff working document SWD(2015)249](https://edz.bib.uni-mannheim.de/edz/pdf/swd/2015/swd-2015-0249-en.pdf) [OTHER (EU-wide; Norway is EEA, not necessarily sampled)][S]
- **Norway: Finansportalen.** Run by **Forbrukerrådet**, launched in **2008** as a collaboration between Finanstilsynet, Forbrukerombudet and Forbrukerrådet. Financial institutions are **obliged to report prices and terms** to it. Commercial card-comparison sites cite Finansportalen as a data source to build credibility. — [Store norske leksikon](https://snl.no/Finansportalen); [kredittkortlisten.no "om oss"](https://www.kredittkortlisten.no/om-oss/) [NO][S]
- **Norway: poor decision-making as a risk marker.** Huse's thesis finds **persistent poor financial decision-making** is associated with higher card delinquency risk (Norwegian bank data). This is indirect evidence that capability and self-efficacy differences matter. — [NHH Open Access](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2640771) [NO][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: INFERRED for the general pattern (high stated trust together with low understanding of business models and heavy reliance on rank), medium confidence**, because the mechanisms are generic to comparison sites. **HYPOTHESIS** for Norwegian trust levels and commission awareness specifically; there is no Norwegian survey on trust in commercial credit-comparison sites.
- Norway-specific HYPOTHESIS: the existence of an official, mandatory-reporting comparator (Finansportalen) sets a credibility benchmark. A commercial service may gain credibility by citing and aligning with Finansportalen data, and lose it if its rankings visibly diverge without explanation.
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Disclose, prominently and in plain Norwegian: **how the service earns money** (commission per application or approval), **which issuers are and are not covered**, **how the ranking is computed** and whether paid placement affects it, **data source and update date**, how personal data are used, and **how to complain**.
  - Avoid unqualified "uavhengig" (independent) or "best" claims (HYPOTHESIS: marketing-law risk).
  - For low self-efficacy users, give one clear next step per screen and plain-language explanations (e.g., what "effektiv rente" includes).

### Gaps
- No Norwegian survey on trust in commercial credit-comparison sites or on awareness of commission was found.
- No evaluation of Finansportalen usage or effects was found.
- No credit-card-specific financial self-efficacy study was retrieved; Lown's (2011) self-efficacy scale and related work were not verified.
- CMA percentages (e.g., share aware of commission, share assuming the top rank is cheapest) were not retrieved.

## 10. Social norms, identity, shame and risk perception around debt

### Takeaway
Norway has direct evidence of a **moral hierarchy of debt**: consumer loans are stigmatised even though card credit is pricier. Young debtors describe **classed shame**, with debt arising from obligations, temptations and expectations. Register data show interest-bearing unsecured debt is strongly associated with enforcement (utleggstrekk). **Norway applicability: stigma and shame VERIFIED (medium-high confidence); peer effects on borrowing INFERRED (low-medium).**

### Cited Findings
- **Norwegian stigma (SIFO 2019).** Consumer loans have the **lowest status** of unsecured products despite being cheapest. Acceptability depends on what the money is used for. — [OsloMet](https://www.oslomet.no/forskning/forskningsnyheter/stor-forbrukslan-skam-i-norge); [ScienceNorway](https://www.sciencenorway.no/consumer-spending-debit-cards-economy/people-are-ashamed-of-their-consumer-loans/1929846) [NO][S]
- **Birkeland & Vogt (2024), "Youth Debt in a Life-Course Context: Young People's Ways into and out of Debt in Norway", *Nordisk Välfärdsforskning* 9(3):238–251, DOI [10.18261/nwr.9.3.3](https://doi.org/10.18261/nwr.9.3.3).** Qualitative biographical interviews with **young debtors aged 25–32** and with **NAV/welfare debt advisers**. Ways **into** debt involve **obligations, temptations and expectations**; ways **out** involve **realising, sharing and waiting**, and realising the problem is a turning point. The authors link debt to **classed shame** about financial mismanagement and to normative, gendered milestones of adulthood. Credit access has grown with deregulation and digital lending. Limitation: small qualitative sample. — [Utdanningsforskning summary](https://utdanningsforskning.no/artikler/2024/youth-debt-in-a-life-course-context-young-peoples-ways-into-and-out-of-debt-in-norway); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f45c517363934854b164766003051ad7) [NO][S]
- **Risk (SIFO-rapport 3-2026: Poppe, Gyüre & Bilgrei, *Gjeldsregistrenes betydning 3.0. Rentebærende, usikret kreditt 2021–2025*).** Register data.
  - In **2025, 171,407 people (4% of the population)** had wage garnishment (utleggstrekk), and **69%** of them had interest-bearing credit.
  - Having interest-bearing unsecured debt is associated with a **ten times higher probability** of utleggstrekk.
  - The probability rises with debt size, to **37.7% above NOK 500,000** in interest-bearing credit.
  - Over 50,000 people have more than NOK 500,000 in unsecured credit.
  - Limitation: associations, not causal.
  — [OsloMet news](https://www.oslomet.no/forskning/forskningsnyheter/gjeldsregisteret-hjelper-bankene-men-oker-presset-pa-lantakere) [NO][S]
- **Lifetime risk.** Hiilamo & Hermansen (2025), using Norwegian national registers: about **a third** of a synthetic cohort experiences payment problems at least once in life, more often men. Norway has the highest household debt burden in the OECD. — [MPIDR working paper / RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:dem:wpaper:wp-2025-006) [NO][S]
- **Distribution (SSB, 2024).** Households held about **NOK 113bn** of interest-bearing unsecured debt, and **nearly 4 in 10** households had some. Only **5.1%** of people had a heavy burden (interest-bearing unsecured debt ≥25% of pre-tax household income). — [SSB "4 av 10 har rentebærende forbruksgjeld"](https://www.ssb.no/inntekt-og-forbruk/inntekt-og-formue/artikler/4-av-10-har-rentebaerende-forbruksgjeld); [SSB "Forbruksgjelda tynger mest for dem med små ressurser"](https://www.ssb.no/inntekt-og-forbruk/artikler-og-publikasjoner/forbruksgjelda-tynger-mest-for-dem-med-sma-ressurser) [NO][S]
- **Who relies on consumer loans (Norges Bank Staff Memo 8/2024).** **Younger age, renting and thin financial assets** go with heavier reliance on consumer loans; **unemployment** pushes households toward unsecured loans to smooth consumption; homeownership lowers reliance. — [Norges Bank](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/publications/Staff-Memo/2024/staff-memo-8-2024-household-accumulation-of-consumer-debt/) [NO][S]
- **Nordic: gambling and impulse spending** precede first high-cost loans in Danish data (Hansen 2022, §1). — [CEBI WP 07/22](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/publikationer/working-papers/CEBI_WP_07-22.rev.pdf) [NORDIC][S]
- **International peer effects.** Dutch survey evidence: people who perceive their social circle's average income as higher borrow more (keeping up). — [Georgarakos, Haliassos & Pasini 2014, "Household Debt and Social Interactions", *RFS* 27(5), DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhu014](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhu014) [OTHER][BK]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: VERIFIED that stigma and shame attach to (some) unsecured debt, medium-high confidence.** How stigma shapes *behaviour* is less measured: it may drive choice of the "less shameful" but costlier card credit (SIFO), and it may delay help-seeking (Birkeland & Vogt's "realising/sharing" stages). Peer and keeping-up effects are **INFERRED (low-medium)**.
- Risk perception: the register evidence (tenfold enforcement risk) gives a strong, Norway-specific, absolute-risk message. The association is not causal, and wording should say "associated with" rather than "causes".
- **Implications for a comparison service:**
  - Use a non-judgmental, normalising tone about *managing* debt ("many people refinance expensive card debt"), never shame.
  - Do not normalise *taking on* debt ("everyone uses credit").
  - Present risk in absolute, Norway-specific numbers.
  - Make help-seeking easy and low-stigma: link to Gjeldsregisteret self-check and to free NAV debt advice, plus a "Struggling to pay?" path separate from product sales.
  - Segment for young renters and low-buffer households, the groups Norges Bank and Birkeland & Vogt identify, with protective defaults.

### Gaps
- SIFO 2019 shame/status percentages and the full SIFO 3-2026 methodology were not retrieved (OsloMet ODA fetch failed).
- No Norwegian study of peer effects on credit card use was found.
- The Forbrukerrådet debt-advice report 2025 ([PDF](https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2026/05/rapport-gjeldsradgivning-2025.pdf)) was located but not read.

## 11. Disclosure design and comparison tools: what helps consumers compare credit products

### Takeaway
The most robust lessons:
- **Fewer, more salient numbers** improve recall and comparison (Danish KFST experiment).
- **Currency-amount costs and total repayable** outperform APR alone (FCA 2026, Bertrand & Morse, ESRI).
- **Defaults in tools steer choices** (ESRI loan calculator; minimum-payment defaults).
- **Pure information disclosures have small effects on debt** (CARD Act: <1% uptake; UK active choice: no debt reduction).

**Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium-high confidence.** The Danish evidence is a close analogue, but no Norwegian disclosure experiment was found.

### Cited Findings
- **KFST (Danish Competition and Consumer Authority) (June 2020), "Consumers benefit from simplified information disclosure".** Behavioural experiment on consumer-credit advertising (simulated TV commercial breaks). With **less information**, consumers were **significantly better at recalling individual elements** and **found it easier to compare and pick the better of two offers**. **Longer run time combined with simpler disclosure** gave the highest recall, and **visual prominence** also helped. The simpler format **cut decision time and effort** without making loans look **more attractive**. Sample sizes and method details were not retrieved. — [KFST publication page](https://en.kfst.dk/publications/2020/20200619-consumers-benefit-from-simplified-information-disclosure); [PDF](https://en.kfst.dk/media/h11nghbg/consumers-benefit-from-simplified-information-disclosure.pdf) [NORDIC][S]
- **KFST (Dec 2018), standardised front page for loan offers.** Consumers benefited from a standardised front page. KFST deliberately chose **monthly payment** as the comparison metric for mortgage offers after finding offers did not clearly show ÅOP or total costs. — [KFST 2018](https://en.kfst.dk/publications/2018/20181219-consumers-benefit-from-a-standardised-front-page-to-loan-offers); [KFST eye-tracking report (iMotions)](https://www.kfst.dk/media/47133/eksperiment-med-standardforside-imotions-afrapportering.pdf) [NORDIC][S]
- **KFST (June 2021): visually salient standardised commercial disclosures on social media benefit consumers.** — [KFST 2021](https://en.kfst.dk/publications/2021/20210617-consumers-benefit-from-visually-salient-standardized-commercial-disclosures-on-social-media) [NORDIC][S]
- **KFST (2023): experiment on a simple summary of a customer's total bank costs**, reported to make banking costs easier to see. — [Danish Ministry of Business fact sheet](https://www.em.dk/Media/639070969103867176/bank_faktaark_1.pdf) [NORDIC][S]
- **FCA (2026): APR plus total amount repayable improves comparability**, and mixed disclosure (APR for some products only) impairs it (§8). — [FCA research note](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/research-note-navigating-disclosure-trade-balancing-flexibility-and-standardisation-cost-credit) [OTHER][S]
- **ESRI (Ireland): online calculator defaults steer loan length.** The loan term consumers chose depended on the **default settings of the online calculator**. — [ESRI "Subtle features of online banking tools can sway consumer choices"](https://www.esri.ie/node/7762); [Irish Times 2019](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/technology/online-tools-can-encourage-consumers-to-pay-more-for-loans-study-finds-1.3986156) [OTHER][S]
- **Dollar-cost disclosure works better than APR in payday lending** (Bertrand & Morse, §8). — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/information-disclosure-cognitive-biases-and-payday-borrowing-united-states) [OTHER][S]
- **Limits of mandated disclosure: CARD Act minimum-payment disclosure** led fewer than 1% of accounts to adopt the suggested payment (Keys & Wang). — [NBER w22742](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22742) [OTHER][S]
- **Choice architecture beats information for repayment**: shroud the minimum option and direct attention to the full balance (Sakaguchi et al. 2022). Active choice changes payment *choices* but not debt (Guttman-Kenney et al. 2025). — [WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/160903/); [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/130080) [OTHER][S]
- **Comparison-site design**: rank position and reviews strongly drive choice; transparency on business model, coverage and complaints is often missing (EU 2014; CMA 2017; §9). — [European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/publications/comparison-tools-and-third-party-verification-schemes_en) [OTHER][S]
- **Norwegian legal baseline**: marketing of credit must include effective rate and a representative example when cost terms are mentioned (Forbrukertilsynet guidance; industry guidance). — [Forbrukertilsynet](https://forbrukertilsynet.no/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/veiledning-til-forskrift-om-markedsforing-av-kreditt.pdf) [NO][S]

### Inferences
- **Norway applicability: INFERRED, medium-high confidence.** Denmark has a similar regulatory culture, high digital adoption and the same EU-derived credit directives (Norway via the EEA; HYPOTHESIS that the implementation details are equivalent). The KFST results are the most transferable evidence for a Norwegian comparison service.
- **Design principles for a comparison service (evidence-led; A/B test them):**
  1. **Card tile** shows at most 4–5 salient items: expected annual cost in NOK for the user's profile, cost of carrying a standard balance (NOK) for 12 months, effective rate (secondary), interest-free days, annual fee. (KFST, FCA.)
  2. **Same scenario for every card**; never show partial cost information for some cards. (FCA.)
  3. **Calculator defaults**: full payment each month and realistic balances. Never default to the minimum payment. (ESRI; Sakaguchi et al.)
  4. **Total repayable** and payoff time shown next to any rate. (FCA; Stewart.)
  5. **Ranking transparency** box on every list. (CMA; EU.)
  6. Expect information alone to have **modest** effects. Pair it with *actions* (set up full-balance autopay, set a limit, refinance-calculator path).

### Gaps
- No Norwegian disclosure or comparison-tool experiment was found.
- CFPB credit card agreement and disclosure testing and the FCA "smarter consumer communications" work were not retrieved.
- KFST sample sizes and effect magnitudes were not retrieved.

## 12. Nordic/Norwegian studies and market context: inventory of what has actually been tested

### Takeaway
Direct Norwegian tests of behavioural mechanisms in credit cards are **scarce**:
- one NHH PhD (decoupling, present bias and delinquency; bank data 2008–2011);
- SIFO surveys and register reports (debt-type stigma; enforcement risk);
- one qualitative youth-debt study;

- Hiilamo & Hermansen (2025): lifetime payment-problem risk about one third (§10). — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:dem:wpaper:wp-2025-006) [S]

A secondary snippet attributes to Norges Bank figures that the share of people over 15 with non-interest-bearing card debt rose from **37% (2022) to almost 48% (2025)**; verify in the primary text. — [Bankplassen 2026](https://www.norges-bank.no/bankplassen/arkiv/2026/dyrtid-og-hoye-renter-har-ikke-fort-til-storre-forbrukslan/) [S]

- SSB (2024): NOK 113bn interest-bearing unsecured debt; ~4 in 10 households; 5.1% heavily burdened (§10). — [SSB](https://www.ssb.no/inntekt-og-forbruk/inntekt-og-formue/artikler/4-av-10-har-rentebaerende-forbruksgjeld) [S]
- Forbrukerrådet Bytteundersøkelsen: about one third switch or renegotiate bank services each year (§7). — [2025 report](https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2025/05/bytteundersokelsen-2025.pdf) [S]
- NHH master's theses: consumer-loan default prediction (10,836 loans at a Norwegian consumer bank; higher rates and more principal-free months raise risk); substitution from mortgages to consumer credit after mortgage tightening. — [NHH 11250/2585551](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2585551); [NHH 11250/2611623](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2611623) [S]

**Nordic evidence [NORDIC]:**
- Denmark: Hansen (2022, CEBI) on present bias in high-cost borrowing; Epper et al. (2020, AER) on patience and wealth; KFST disclosure experiments (2018, 2020, 2021, 2023); the ÅOP cap (2020). (§1, §4, §11)
- Sweden: the 2018 high-cost credit caps, with loans clustering just below the cap; FI reports that **one in seven** [high-cost] loans led to a collection notice (FI consumption-loan report 2019; snippet). — [FI Konsumtionslån 2019 (Eng)](https://fi.se/contentassets/3900398fb47e41d6a6a5d9af378a1f36/konsumtionslan-2019-eng.pdf); [FI-analys 22](https://fi.se/contentassets/b9c6954789a44fa6ab8e0f48faceae47/fi-analys-22-hogkostnadskrediter-engn.pdf) [S]
- Finland: caps (2013, 2019, 2023) and circumvention via larger loans (Bank of Finland). — [BoF Bulletin](https://www.bofbulletin.fi/en/2019/2/new-methods-needed-to-rein-in-consumer-credit/) [S]
- Not found: any Swedish or Finnish *borrower-level* behavioural study of credit cards or payday loans in this session's searches (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- **Segmentation implication (INFERRED, medium-high confidence).** Norwegian card use is increasingly "transactor" behaviour (non-interest-bearing balances rising, interest-bearing card debt falling since the 2019 regulation and debt register). The behavioural mechanisms that harm consumers (present bias, minimum-payment anchoring, teaser naivety, reward-induced spending) therefore apply mainly to a **revolving minority**, possibly about half of card balances by value (Kredinor, commercial). That minority is concentrated among the young, renters, low-asset and unemployed households (Norges Bank) and overlaps with consumer-loan holders (SIFO). Messaging should separate a "payment card" journey (rewards, insurance, fees) from a "borrowing" journey (cost, payoff, refinancing, help).
- Applying US effect sizes directly to Norway would overstate them. Norway has stricter lending rules (2019), a debt register used by lenders, mandatory effective-rate disclosure, and a lower share of consumers revolving.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian randomised experiment on any credit card behavioural mechanism was found.
- No Norwegian present-bias elicitation linked to debt was found.
- Norges Bank Staff Memo 8/2024 and Bankplassen full texts could not be fetched (DNS failure on www.norges-bank.no), so their card-specific numbers are unverified.
- No Swedish or Finnish borrower-level behavioural studies were found; searching in Swedish or Finnish (e.g., "snabblån registerdata", "pikavippi") may help.
- BI and UiO work was not specifically found.

## 13. Synthesis: Norway-applicability classification and communication implications by mechanism

### Takeaway
Only **debt-type mental accounting and stigma** and (weakly) **decoupling and present-bias-related delinquency** have Norwegian empirical support. Disclosure simplification has close **Danish** experimental support. Everything else is **INFERRED** from UK and US evidence or remains a **HYPOTHESIS**. The comparison service's highest-confidence actions:
- lead with NOK costs and total repayable;
- default to full repayment;
- rank by user-profile expected cost;
- be transparent about the business model and ranking;
- use non-shaming language with a separate help path.

### Cited Findings
- Supporting evidence is cited in §1–12. The key anchors: Norwegian stigma and paradox ([OsloMet/SIFO](https://www.oslomet.no/forskning/forskningsnyheter/stor-forbrukslan-skam-i-norge)); Norwegian decoupling and delinquency ([NHH Huse 2019](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2640771)); Danish present bias in high-cost loans ([CEBI WP 07/22](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/publikationer/working-papers/CEBI_WP_07-22.rev.pdf)); Danish simplified disclosure ([KFST 2020](https://en.kfst.dk/publications/2020/20200619-consumers-benefit-from-simplified-information-disclosure)); UK APR and total repayable ([FCA 2026](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/research-note-navigating-disclosure-trade-balancing-flexibility-and-standardisation-cost-credit)); UK minimum-payment defaults ([Sakaguchi et al. 2022](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/160903/)); UK de-anchoring without debt reduction ([Guttman-Kenney et al. 2025](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/130080)); Norwegian enforcement risk ([SIFO 3-2026 via OsloMet](https://www.oslomet.no/forskning/forskningsnyheter/gjeldsregisteret-hjelper-bankene-men-oker-presset-pa-lantakere)).

- **Overall caution (labelled generalisation).** Effect sizes come mostly from US/UK samples with higher revolving rates and looser regulation than Norway. They should be treated as upper bounds for Norway, and no mechanism should be described as applying to "Norwegian consumers" in general. The evidence supports describing segments: transactors vs revolvers; young, renter, low-buffer households; consumers holding several unsecured debts.
- **Ethical design note (INFERRED).** Several mechanisms (present bias, anchoring, rewards, rank-position effects) can be exploited as easily as countered. Because the comparison service's revenue (commission on applications) may reward volume, the credibility findings (§9) suggest transparency is both an ethical and a commercial necessity.
